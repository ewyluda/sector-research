## Purpose

Variant Perception is the alpha theory of the investment thesis. It answers the question: **why does this opportunity exist?** If the thesis agrees with consensus on every dimension, there is no edge — the stock is efficiently priced at fair value and the expected return equals cost of capital.

Alpha requires disagreeing with the market and being right. This sub-skill forces the analyst to be specific about both halves of that statement: exactly where is the disagreement, and what evidence supports the variant view over the consensus view?

The source of excess return in investing is not information advantage (that is increasingly illegal or structurally unavailable). It is **analytical advantage** — processing the same information differently, with a longer time horizon, a different reference class, or a more rigorous causal model. Variant Perception makes the analytical advantage explicit so it can be challenged, refined, and ultimately validated or refuted.

This sub-skill is qualitative. It is not scored. A variant perception that is right generates returns. One that is wrong generates losses. The quality criteria are specificity and falsifiability — not polish.

## Methodology

### Step 1: Articulate the Consensus View

Before stating where the thesis differs from consensus, state what consensus believes. Consensus is not "the market is wrong" — it is a specific claim about what the current price implies.

**Sources for consensus:**
- Analyst price targets and ratings (identify the median target, the bull/bear range, and the most common rating)
- Earnings estimates (what does the Street expect for the next 4-8 quarters on key metrics: revenue, EBITDA, EPS, FCF)
- Published analyst reports (identify the 2-3 most commonly cited bull and bear arguments from sell-side coverage)
- Implied growth rate (reverse-engineer the DCF: what growth rate is the current stock price implying, given current multiples and discount rate assumptions)
- Short interest and put/call ratio (what is the market's current directional bet)

**Summarize consensus in three elements:**
1. **Consensus narrative**: What is the dominant story the market tells about this company? (1-2 sentences)
2. **Consensus price target**: What return does the median analyst expect, and over what horizon?
3. **What is priced in**: What growth rate, margin profile, or outcome does the current stock price require to be fair-valued?

### Step 2: Articulate the Thesis View

State the investment thesis view on the same dimensions as consensus. This is not a re-statement of the bull case from Bull/Bear Framing — it is specifically the ways the thesis view differs from the consensus narrative, price target, and implied assumptions.

For each dimension where the thesis differs from consensus:
- State the consensus position explicitly
- State the thesis position explicitly
- State the magnitude of the disagreement (e.g., "Consensus expects 12% revenue CAGR over 3 years; this thesis assumes 18-22% CAGR driven by international expansion that the Street has not yet modeled")

### Step 3: Identify the Specific Disagreement

The variant perception is the single most important place where the thesis view differs from consensus — the disagreement that, if the thesis is right, explains most of the potential return.

**Variant Perception Statement format:**

"The market believes [consensus claim]. This thesis believes [variant claim]. The disagreement is specific to [dimension: a metric, a competitive dynamic, a time horizon, a causal mechanism]. If the market's view is right, the stock is [fairly valued / overvalued / undervalued at current price]. If the thesis view is right, the stock is worth [target] within [time horizon]."

**Common types of variant perception:**
- **Time horizon variant**: Consensus is focused on near-term headwinds; thesis focuses on 3-5 year structural opportunity that is not yet reflected in estimates
- **Metric variant**: Consensus tracks the wrong metric (e.g., tracking GAAP EPS when FCF per share is the more predictive measure of value creation)
- **Competitive dynamics variant**: Consensus assumes intensifying competition; thesis believes the company's moat is more durable than consensus models
- **Transition variant**: Consensus is pricing the company as a legacy business; thesis believes the transition to a new model (SaaS, services, AI-native) will re-rate the multiple
- **Capital allocation variant**: Consensus ignores management's deployment of capital; thesis believes a specific allocation (buyback, acquisition, capex cut) creates significant per-share value
- **Macro sensitivity variant**: Consensus assumes the company is highly rate-sensitive or cyclical; thesis believes the business model has less macro sensitivity than implied
- **Category error variant**: Consensus is valuing the company in the wrong peer group; re-categorization to the correct peer group implies a different multiple

One variant is sufficient. More than two variants in a single thesis suggests the analyst has not identified the primary driver and is producing a list rather than an argument.

### Step 4: Assess Evidence For and Against

The variant perception is not a conclusion — it is a hypothesis. The analyst must assess the evidence on both sides.

**Evidence For the Variant View:**
- List 3-5 specific findings from prior due diligence categories that support the variant perception
- For each, state the category, sub-skill, and specific data point (same citation format as Evidence Grounding)
- Order from strongest to weakest evidence

**Evidence Against the Variant View (devil's advocate):**
- List 2-3 specific findings or risk factors that support the consensus view or challenge the variant
- Be honest — if the evidence against is strong, the probability estimate on the bull case should reflect that
- State explicitly what would make the variant perception wrong: "If [observable event] occurs, it would confirm that the consensus view is correct and the variant perception is invalid."

**Evidence verdict**: After reviewing both sides, state whether the evidence supports the variant perception at LOW / MEDIUM / HIGH confidence. This is not a score — it is a qualitative commitment that the analyst can be held to.

## Key Questions

1. If a sophisticated investor read this thesis and the consensus research, what would they identify as the key disagreement?
2. Is the variant perception based on information the market does not have, or on a different interpretation of publicly available information? (The latter is the only legal and repeatable source of edge.)
3. Can the variant perception be proven wrong by a single observable event or data point? If not, it is not specific enough.
4. How long has the market held the consensus view, and what would cause the consensus to change? (A consensus that has held for years requires a specific catalyst to shift — this links back to Catalyst Identification.)
5. Is this variant perception original, or have other investors published the same view? (If widely published, it may already be priced in — the variant perception itself needs a variant.)
6. What is the market's mechanism for discovering that the variant view is correct? (If there is no mechanism — no earnings report, no product release, no regulatory event — the stock cannot re-rate even if the thesis is right.)
7. Has the company itself acknowledged the disconnect between its view and consensus? Management commentary that explicitly addresses the variant dimension is strong supporting evidence.

## Red Flags

- Variant perception is "the market underappreciates this company" — not specific; every bull thesis says this without saying where or why
- No consensus price target or estimate reviewed — analyst stated a variant view without establishing what consensus actually believes
- Variant perception requires information that is not publicly available — this is either an information edge that is illegal or a claim that cannot be validated
- The disagreement is on a metric that does not affect valuation (e.g., "consensus uses the wrong definition of revenue" without explaining why the correct definition changes the stock's fair value)
- Evidence for the variant view is entirely management guidance and company presentations (Tier 3) — no independent validation
- No falsification criteria stated — cannot identify what observable event would prove the variant perception wrong
- The variant perception has been published by multiple prominent investors for 2+ years without re-rating — the market has heard it and disagrees; the analyst must explain why the market is still wrong
- Variant perception changes direction mid-document — bull thesis and bear catalyst section imply different variants; the analyst is not committed to a coherent view

## Source Requirements

- **Bull/Bear Framing output**: Required. The variant perception must be consistent with the primary claim of the bull case.
- **Sell-side analyst reports**: Required for articulating consensus. At minimum, review 3-5 reports covering the stock. Sources: Bloomberg, Refinitiv, company investor relations page (some companies post sell-side coverage summaries).
- **Consensus earnings estimates**: Required. Sources: FactSet, Bloomberg, Refinitiv, Seeking Alpha earnings estimate page.
- **Implied growth rate calculation**: Required for "what is priced in" section. Requires current stock price, normalized earnings, and a discount rate assumption.
- **Short interest and options data**: Optional but informative for understanding the directional bet the market is making. Sources: FINRA short interest data, CBOE options data.

## Output

- Consensus statement: narrative (1-2 sentences), median price target, implied growth rate / what is priced in
- Thesis view: stated on same dimensions as consensus, with explicit delta on each dimension
- Variant Perception Statement: single paragraph in specified format (market believes → thesis believes → disagreement dimension → stock value in each scenario)
- Variant type classification: time horizon / metric / competitive dynamics / transition / capital allocation / macro sensitivity / category error
- Evidence For (3-5 points with category/sub-skill citations)
- Evidence Against (2-3 points with explicit falsification criteria)
- Evidence verdict: LOW / MEDIUM / HIGH confidence
- Variant perception status: UNPRICED (market has not yet moved toward thesis view) / PRICING IN (market is beginning to move) / PRICED (re-rating has occurred; monitor for next variant)
